Provisional manifest audit

Literal status

The first complete research ledger contains 220 positions in the exact block allocation promised by the coverage matrix. It is a candidate curriculum, not an accepted tracklist.

  • 220 provisional rows
  • 0 accepted rows
  • 3 release records verified against artist- or label-controlled Bandcamp pages
  • 0 SoundCloud recordings resolved
  • 57 rows presently tagged transnational or non-US
  • 37 rows explicitly tagged for women participants
  • 5 rows explicitly tagged for queer participation
  • 30 rows presently tagged for a non-MC role or live practice

The representation figures are audit signals derived from tags. They are not demographic truth and do not prove adequate treatment.

Repairs made after the first challenge pass

The first full list exposed omissions that the twelve-block outline concealed. The following additions replaced redundant or weaker teaching jobs:

  • Cypress Hill and Vico C strengthened Latino Los Angeles, Puerto Rican, Caribbean, and Spanish-language continuity.
  • Bone Thugs-N-Harmony repaired the absence of a major Midwestern melodic-flow lineage.
  • Master P added the No Limit business and roster system.
  • DMX and Eminem added distinct late-1990s performance, narrative, and market transformations.
  • J. Cole and Tyler, the Creator repaired two conspicuous 2010s absences.
  • Control Machete, Tego Calderón, Ana Tijoux, Canserbero, and Barkaa expanded the global route beyond the strongest existing French, British, African, and Brazilian clusters.
  • Yo-Yo, Boss, Rah Digga, Jean Grae, Rye Rye, Ana Tijoux, Angel Haze, and Cardi B raised women-tagged positions from 30 to 37 while adding distinct scenes or techniques.
  • Pop Smoke made the reciprocal UK-to-Brooklyn drill circuit audible.

Metadata-assisted challenge pass

A read-only MusicBrainz search generated candidate records for 202 of 220 positions. Of those, 155 returned an exact normalized title and artist within one year of the expected release date. Seventeen archival, regional, multilingual, or platform-era rows returned no candidate. One query still returns a temporary service error after retries.

These are discovery results, not version verification. MusicBrainz often associates a recording entry with a later compilation or reissue date; early break records and canonical rap singles therefore produced conspicuous false date conflicts. The candidate layer is valuable for locating records and exposing disagreements, but it cannot select the intended master.

The pass did expose three errors in the curriculum itself. They have been repaired and verified against artist- or label-controlled Bandcamp pages:

  • MIKE’s 2017 “Hunger” was wrongly attached to Beware of the Monkey. It is now the actual 2022 album track “Nuthin I can do is Wrng.”
  • Kneecap’s older “C.E.A.R.T.A.” was wrongly described as a 2024 Fine Art recording. It is now “Sick In The Head.”
  • MC Yallah’s 2019 Kubali title was wrongly attached to the 2023 album Yallah Beibe. It is now “Sikwebela,” credited with producer Debmaster.

Findings that still block acceptance

  1. Release metadata is mostly unverified. Two hundred seventeen rows still require a release-level source for year, credit, title, label issue, mix, and requested master. Several rows deliberately name known version traps: “Push It,” “Fight the Power,” “Tha Crossroads,” “Day ‘N’ Nite,” early French recordings, mixtape originals, and archival party tapes.
  2. The live-culture block is archival rather than playlist-ready. Cornell catalog identifiers establish that recordings exist, but access, carrier identity, duration, excerpt policy, and SoundCloud availability remain unresolved.
  3. Global coverage is broad but temporally uneven. African, Asian, Latin American, and Indigenous selections become more numerous late in the chronology. Research must distinguish genuine historical scarcity in the available recording route from the easier discovery of platform-era material.
  4. The list remains MC-centered. Thirty non-MC/live tags are a start, but DJ, dancer, engineer, radio, and organizer histories will depend heavily on prose, images, tapes, and map nodes rather than credited tracks.
  5. Queer history is under-described. Five explicit tags almost certainly undercount participation and audience history. Identity claims require reliable sourcing and should not be inferred from performance style.
  6. Famous works risk doing lazy work. “Rapper’s Delight,” “The Message,” “Planet Rock,” “Fight the Power,” Illmatic, The Chronic, and several later hits are potentially clichéd because surveys repeat them reflexively. They remain provisional only where their teaching job is indispensable; the finished annotations must say why the obvious choice survived stronger alternatives.
  7. Some major omissions remain arguable. Scarface as a solo artist, Busta Rhymes, Big Pun, Fugees, Roc Marciano, Nipsey Hussle, Megan Thee Stallion, and several national scenes could all support serious objections. They should be tested against existing rows rather than added by expanding the route indiscriminately.
  8. The unfinished present is especially unstable. Current selections can demonstrate live branches but cannot yet support settled influence claims.

Acceptance rule

A row may move from provisional to accepted only when its teaching job survives comparison with alternatives, its exact intended recording is specified, its release metadata has a source, and its inclusion does not create an unacknowledged coverage distortion. SoundCloud availability is a later implementation gate and cannot decide historical worth by itself.
