Comparison exercises

Do not try to memorize dozens of labels. After the chronological pass, use these comparisons to convert names into audible distinctions:

Several exercises intentionally retain historically important recordings whose exact requested versions are not currently playable on Spotify. Check the Spotify link index and catalog-gap notes before listening; a nearby edit or remix can change the very feature the exercise asks you to hear.

  1. What changed when house split? Compare “Move Your Body,” “Mystery of Love,” “Acid Tracks,” “To Be in Love,” “Revolution 909,” “Easy Lee,” “Superman,” and “Losing It.” Keep the four-on-the-floor reference pulse constant in your mind while noting what each scene treats as the main event.
  2. How can one break become many genres? Move from “Mr Kirk’s Nightmare” through “We Are I.E.,” “Original Nuttah,” “Horizons,” “Brown Paper Bag,” “Bacteria,” “Mr Maverick,” “Stigma,” and “Baianá.” Focus first on break treatment, then bass, then the imagined dancer.
  3. Where did the kick go? Play “RipGroove,” the Kelly G remix of “Never Gonna Let You Go,” “Destiny,” “Battle,” “Gorgon Sound,” “Midnight Request Line,” and “Archangel.” Clap quarter notes while the programmed kick increasingly refuses them.
  4. What does “progressive” mean? Compare “Xpander,” “One,” and “Levels.” The first develops by slow metamorphosis; the later records optimize large-scale collective cues. Similar labels do not guarantee the same compositional time.
  5. How does extreme speed organize differently? Compare “Now Is the Time,” “Hajnal,” “Linga Linga,” and “Tsekeleke.” Happy hardcore, breakcore, singeli, and Shangaan electro can all feel fast while differing in breaks, social dance, phrase structure, and genealogy.
  6. Programmed rhythm as performance. Watch or listen closely to “Spastik,” “Horizons,” “Brown Paper Bag,” “Baby Come On,” “Kahraba,” “Ice Drop,” and “Black Origami.” Ask the same questions one might ask of a drummer—independence, accent hierarchy, subdivision, tension, release—without assuming a human kit is the norm against which the programming must be judged.
  7. How does one bass continuum keep branching? Compare “Super Sharp Shooter,” “Mr Happy,” “Low Blow,” and “Told You (Soundboy).” Follow the change from chopped hip-hop/jungle exuberance through talking jump-up bass to the long foghorn note and back toward clash-culture vocals.
  8. What happened after brostep? Compare “Midnight Request Line,” “Anti War Dub,” “Scary Monsters and Nice Sprites,” “Orgalorg,” “Casket,” and “Skanka.” Listen for how sub-bass, midrange aggression, repetition, silence, and festival-scale impact are recombined rather than treating post-2010 dubstep as one linear escalation.
  9. What does dembow mean here? Compare “Under Mi Sleng Teng,” “Tu Pun Pun,” “Gasolina,” “Sandungueo,” and “El Alma Que Te Trajo.” Distinguish a Jamaican reusable riddim, the characteristic reggaetón drum cell, Dominican dembow as a later genre name, and contemporary mutations of perreo rhythm.
  10. What does “garage” mean here? Start with the Peech Boys’ “Don’t Make Me Wait,” then compare “Devotion,” “Saved My Life,” “To Be in Love,” “RipGroove,” the Kelly G remix of “Never Gonna Let You Go,” and “Destiny.” The first group moves through the New York/New Jersey club lineage and its overlap with Chicago vocal house; the second follows the British reworking from speed garage into 2-step. Track four features separately: vocal and gospel emphasis, dub space, drum swing, and sub-bass. The relative weight of those features changes even when the family name persists.
