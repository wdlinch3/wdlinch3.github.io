IX. Global club plurality and revival, 2015–2022

The present is not one post-genre blur. Local rhythmic systems remain audible while tracks circulate globally; older forms such as acid techno, jungle, trance, and garage revive without returning unchanged. Several labels here—hard drum, post-footwork, neoperreo—remain provisional or contested. They are used as navigation aids, never as claims that artists belong in one box.

103. UNIIQU3 — “Yo (I’m Lit)” (original version, 2015) — Jersey club, Newark. Rapid vocal chops, MC energy, and the genre’s kick pattern demonstrate Jersey club as a living local form rather than a pop-production garnish. Listen for: the beat issuing repeated dance cues at a pace that rewards instant bodily response.

104. Islam Chipsy & EEK — “Kahraba” (album version, 2015) — Egyptian shaabi-derived keyboard-and-live-drum performance, Cairo. Chipsy’s keyboards spar with two live drummers in an ecstatic feedback loop. Chipsy rejected identification with mahraganat/electro-chaabi; this selection represents a neighboring live wedding-performance tradition. This is the survey’s most direct bridge to live-drum analysis, but its purpose is to represent Egyptian practice, not to validate electronics through acoustic virtuosity. Spotify availability: the exact requested version is not currently playable in the authenticated United States catalog and is omitted from the 146-track Spotify edition. Rhythm lens: track how drummer fills provoke keyboard flourishes and vice versa.

105. DJ Lag — “Ice Drop” (original version, 2016) — gqom, Durban. Ominous pads, clipped percussion, and an implied-but-missing four-on-the-floor pulse teach negative space at body scale. Listen for: kicks arriving asymmetrically while the groove remains locally coherent.

106. Jlin — “Black Origami” (album version, 2017) — post-footwork/rhythmic abstraction, Gary. Jlin turns footwork-related language into intricate, ensemble-like polyrhythm without using dance battle function as a limitation. Rhythm lens: choose one metallic timbre as a “limb,” follow it through changing groupings, then reconstruct the composite meter.

107. Charlotte de Witte — “Sgadi Li Mi” (original version, 2020) — contemporary acid/hard techno, Ghent. Acid detail and warehouse pressure show techno’s late-2010s harder revival in a compact, legible form. It is not offered as the genre’s new endpoint; it is a modern contrast with Phuture, Hardfloor, and Hood. Listen for: how the acid voice is subordinated to kick-driven mass compared with “Acid Tracks.”

108. Kabza De Small feat. Leehleza — “Umshove” (original version, 2018) — amapiano, South Africa. Patient form, deep/jazz-house atmosphere, and the emerging log-drum language document an early breakthrough without naming one inventor. Amapiano’s origins are collective and disputed. Listen for: the bass entering as a tuned, sliding drum voice rather than a conventional sustained line.

109. Bamba Pana — “Linga Linga” (album version, 2018) — singeli, Dar es Salaam. Hyper-fast loop construction leaves room for MC cadence and social dance. It is an essential East African core selection and an excellent contrast with both happy hardcore and breakcore. Listen for: a very fast surface organized around repeating vocal and rhythmic cells rather than break slicing.

110. DJ Plead — “Salt and Pepper” (original version, 2019) — hard drum/percussive club. Lebanese-Australian rhythmic vocabulary meets UK bass architecture. “Hard drum” is treated as a loose late-2010s tendency, but the track’s percussion-first design makes that tendency easy to hear. Listen for: darbuka-related gestures translated into synthetic, low-end-heavy club space.

111. FISHER — “Losing It” (original version, 2018) — festival tech house. An ultra-legible bass hook and build/drop economy show tech house’s mass-festival form. Canon/cliché note: the track became a catch-all reference for a much larger tech-house field because one bass phrase and one payoff are unusually memorable; use it to hear the mass-festival formula, not to define the whole genre. Listen for: how much of the arrangement exists to reset attention on one bass phrase.

112. MFR Souls feat. DJ Maphorisa, Kabza De Small & Sha Sha — “Love You Tonight” (original version, 2019) — amapiano, South Africa. Log drum, shakers, patient harmonic cycling, and songful vocals show the style in a fuller mature form after “Umshove.” Listen for: percussion and bass establishing multiple simultaneous depths rather than one foreground beat.

113. Nia Archives — “Baianá” (original version, 2022) — new-jungle revival, United Kingdom. Jungle breaks and a Brazilian vocal sample make the revival immediately attractive while raising questions about sample travel and context. It belongs here because revival is audible as both historical memory and contemporary song economy. Listen for: old-school break pressure compressed into a concise modern arrangement.

114. Safety Trance feat. Arca — “El Alma Que Te Trajo” (original version, 2022) — neoperreo/queer Latin club. Reggaetón-derived rhythm is made abrasive, erotic, and explicitly queer through industrial sound design. It follows rather than replaces origin-grounded Latin forms in the route. Listen for: dembow continuity surviving severe changes in timbre and vocal presentation.

Historical orientation: gqom scene report; South African music-rights organization discussion of amapiano; singeli producer profile.
